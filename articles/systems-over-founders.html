Systems over founders

Key-person dependency caps enterprise value. Boards can design it out years before a sale.

By Geoff Bamber · Draft

Buyers pay for cash flows that will survive a change of ownership. When those cash flows depend on one person they price the risk. Valuation advisers call this the key-person discount. William Buck, an Australian accounting and advisory firm, put the typical range at 10% to 25% of enterprise value in January 2025.

The discount rarely appears as a line in the offer letter. It shows up as a lower multiple, a larger share of the price deferred into an earn-out, a requirement for the founder to roll equity and stay for years, or a bidder who drops out after the management presentation.

Founder-led businesses are the natural home of the problem. Pricing, the largest customer relationships, hiring judgement and the numbers all sit in one head. Excellence makes the dependency worse because nobody else has been asked to develop the same judgement.

Professionalising after investment

I was the first chief executive appointed after private equity invested in London Gynaecology, a founder-led private healthcare group. Professionalising the group meant moving knowledge out of individual heads and into processes, reporting and a management team the new owners could rely on.

Designing myself out

At Clava I applied the same discipline to my own role. I founded the business and ran it as chief executive from 2022 to 2024. I then recruited a successor from the climbing industry and became chairman. Today I sit on the board as a non-executive director. Clava runs without me day to day. For a buyer or an investor that is the strongest evidence I can offer that its value does not leave with me.

Four steps a board can take now

  1. Decision map. List the decisions only the founder makes today, from pricing to senior hires. Name who will make each one in two years.
  2. Customer relationships. Give every top-ten customer a second senior contact and record each relationship in the CRM.
  3. Reporting. Build a monthly pack the board trusts without the founder in the room to explain the numbers.
  4. Absence test. Send the founder away for four consecutive weeks. Every call they receive marks a missing process.

I would run the absence test at least two years before a planned sale. That leaves time to fix the gaps before a buyer’s diligence team finds them.
