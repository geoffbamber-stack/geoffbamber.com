Systems over founders

Key-person dependency caps enterprise value. Thoughtful boards design it out years before an exit.

By Geoff Bamber · 15 November 2026

Buyers pay for cash flows that will survive a change of ownership. When those cash flows depend on one person they price the risk associated with that individual. Valuation advisers call this the key-person discount. William Buck, an Australian accounting and advisory firm, put the typical range at 10% to 25% of enterprise value in January 2025.

The discount rarely appears as a separate line in the offer letter. It usually shows up as a lower multiple, a larger share of the price deferred into an earn-out, a requirement for the founder to roll equity and stay for years, or a bidder who withdraws after the management presentation and gives only vague reasons.

Founder-led businesses are the natural breeding ground for this problem. Pricing, the largest customer relationships, hiring judgement and the numbers all sit in one head.

A visionary founder who has driven a company’s success creates a tension for any buyer. Losing the founder is a risk; relying on the founder limits the upside. Excellence makes the dependency worse because nobody else has been asked, or felt the need, to develop the same judgement.

Professionalising after investment

I was the first chief executive appointed after private equity invested in London Gynaecology, a founder-led private healthcare group. Professionalising the group meant creating processes, reporting and a management team fit for a PE-backed business.

Designing myself out

At Clava I applied the same discipline to my own role. I founded the business and ran it as chief executive from 2022 to 2024. I then recruited a successor from the climbing industry and became chairman. Today I sit on the board as a non-executive director. Clava runs without me day to day. For a buyer or an investor that is the strongest evidence I can offer that its value does not reside with me.

Practical steps a board can take now

  1. Decision map. List the decisions the founder makes or will not let go of today, from pricing to senior hires to HR. Agree who will make each one in two years and the evidence that will show the founder has stepped back.
  2. Key commercial relationships. Give every top-ten customer and supplier a second senior contact and record each relationship in the CRM. Hand each relationship over properly and systematically. Buyers spot a token handover very quickly.
  3. Reporting. Build a monthly pack the board trusts without the founder in the room to explain the numbers, reports or issues. Count how often the management team look to the founder to answer a question in board meetings.
  4. Absence test. Send the founder away for four consecutive weeks. If hiring stops, decisions wait for the founder’s return, or product and pricing choices go on ice, the business still has a key-person problem.

I would run the absence test at least two years before a planned sale. That leaves time to fix the gaps before a buyer’s diligence team finds them.

Boards should also watch for three cues in the room:

  • Management team holding back to avoid angering or embarrassing the founder
  • Decisions that contradict earlier boardroom discussion
  • Action points that take unusually long to move forward

Sources
