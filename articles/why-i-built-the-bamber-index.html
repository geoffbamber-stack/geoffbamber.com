Bamber Index

I built the Bamber Global Prestige Index to replace vibes with data

Wealth is counted everywhere but nobody measures the places where it chooses to invest and be seen.

By Geoff Bamber · 15 November 2026

During my years in hedge funds I met a number of billionaires. Their choices away from the desk fascinated me: the jets they flew, the resorts they returned to each winter and the coastlines where they bought second homes. People with essentially unlimited resources kept choosing the same small set of places. Iceman in Top Gun wanted to know who the best pilot was. I wanted to know which place is the most prestigious.

Just as water flows downhill, wealth flows to prestige. A Geneva banker does not enjoy February in St Moritz for the tax treaty. He goes for the Cresta Run, the bar at Badrutt’s Palace and the company on the terrace at Corviglia. Choices like his reveal more about prestige than any wealth table.

Everything else about wealth is measured obsessively. UBS counted nearly a million new dollar millionaires in 2025, about 2,600 a day. Henley & Partners forecast that 142,000 millionaires would move country that year. Those figures follow fortunes, residency and tax. None says which places money chooses to enjoy, invest in and be seen in once it is free to go anywhere.

When I went looking for that answer I found hotel awards, readers’ polls and wealth rankings with a lifestyle score bolted on. None of those would survive contact with a board or C-suite looking to justify a serious investment decision.

Prestige gets asserted all the same. People regularly declare that one place is more prestigious than another: St Moritz than Courchevel, Monaco than Dubai. Few can say what backs those assertions up, mainly because nothing reliable is available.

Large decisions get made on that thin basis. A luxury brand picking its next boutique, a sponsor choosing a host city, a family office deciding on a second home and an events business weighing two resorts all tend to work from reputation, a director’s last holiday and a stack of glossy travel lists. Call it vibes. Poor vibes-based decisions can be expensive mistakes, both for the money they lose you and for the better option you passed over.

So I decided to build the measure I couldn’t find because it didn’t exist. The Bamber Global Prestige Index substantiates the claim that one place is more prestigious than another rigorously and independently. Each and every score has been carefully sourced, measured and stress-tested. I wanted brands, event organisers, corporates and families to be able to put this index in front of a board or their bosses and colleagues.

The Index now scores 161 destinations on equally weighted pillars. Reputation, exclusion, and heritage and momentum are among its dimensions. Its markers are the traces wealth leaves when it is routinely and reliably present: for example, boutiques of the ultra high-end watch houses, private jet movements and superyacht berthing fees.

Raw counts favour big cities. Because London and New York will always have more of everything, the headline ranking adjusts for size. A village such as Gstaad can then compete with a capital of nine million on the density of what it offers.

The practical uses follow from there. A watch house can see which cities already draw its clients before choosing the site of its next boutique. An events business such as LeBlanq, organiser of luxury cycling events, can test which destination to add to its calendar next. For a corporate the Index can settle the city for a leadership retreat, or for a regional office its senior hires would be glad to live in. Families deciding where to spend their summers get evidence in place of anecdote.

The Index is published on 24 November 2026. Its first 50 destinations will be available in full at bamberindex.com; the complete edition will come as a PDF and in 50 copies bound by hand in London.
